A website is handed over when your client can change it without calling you. That depends less on code and more on how understandable the schema is, how the site behaves with real content, and whether they know the edit-preview-publish loop.

## Make the schema readable

Your client sees definition and field names, descriptions and limits. So does the AI assistant, which uses them to make sensible edits.

- **Name things the way your client does.** "Services", not `offerings_v2`.
- **Describe where each field appears.** `"Shown in bold under the photo on the homepage."`
- **Set real limits.** If a heading breaks the layout past 60 characters, use `max: 60`.
- **Mark optional things optional,** and make sure the page looks fine without them.
- **Hide technical choices.** No raw HTML, CSS classes or IDs. Offer an `s.enum()` with readable values instead.
- **Remove fields that do nothing.** Every field should visibly change something on the site.

Changing names later is possible but needs a [migration](/guides/schema-changes/), so it pays to get them right before handover.

## Test with realistic content

Placeholder text hides problems. Before handover, fill the site with the real thing, then try the awkward cases:

- the longest heading the limits allow,
- an entry without its optional image,
- an empty collection, and one with many entries,
- every page on a phone.

## Walk through one complete edit together

<Steps>

1. Ask your client to change a headline in the dashboard.

2. Have them click **Preview** and find the change on the site. Point out that nothing is live yet.

3. Have them publish. On AtMyApp hosting, show them the build finishing and the change appearing a minute or two later.

4. Repeat with an image, a new collection entry and, if the site has one, a form submission in the inbox.

5. Show them the AI assistant: "Add a new service called Catering with a short description." It prepares the change as a draft; they review and publish it.

</Steps>

## Leave a short handover note

Write down, in plain language:

- the dashboard address and the live website address,
- which dashboard section edits which part of the site,
- how long a published change takes to appear,
- where form messages arrive and who gets notified,
- who to contact when something looks wrong.

Keep credentials out of the note. Your client signs in with their own account; CLI keys stay with you.

## Before you leave

- [ ] Every editable field has a description and sensible limits.
- [ ] Realistic content is published, and the site handles missing optional values.
- [ ] Previews work, and the live site shows only published content.
- [ ] Forms arrive in the inbox, notifications go to the right people, and **Allowed websites** is set.
- [ ] Website settings (name, description, sharing image, icon) are filled in.
- [ ] Your client has published at least one change themselves.
